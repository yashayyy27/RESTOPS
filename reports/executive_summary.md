# RESTOPS · Executive summary

**Southern Table Hospitality | January 2024–December 2025 | Synthetic portfolio case study**

The validated operation contains 794,925 orders across 18 restaurants. Revenue was A$36,444,594, operating profit A$4,386,880, and operating margin 12.0%.

These are fictional outcomes. Recommendations are testable management hypotheses; observational estimates do not establish causal business effects.

## 1. Prioritise the weakest store's cost structure

**What happened:** Wollongong recorded 1.8% operating margin in Q4 versus 11.1% across its location peers.

**Why:** Its labour cost was 46.2% of sales and waste was 7.7%. These are accounting drivers; store and demand differences still matter.

**Business impact:** Q4 operating profit was A$3,965 on A$216,409 sales. A peer-margin gap is an investigation signal, not guaranteed recoverable profit.

**Recommended action:** Area Manager: review four weeks of rosters and waste logs, then pilot a demand-based roster. Monitor margin and service scores together.

## 2. Move staffing toward meal peaks

**What happened:** 43.5% of peak-hour observations exceeded modelled service capacity; 65.4% of off-peak observations had spare capacity.

**Why:** Broad shifts spread paid hours across the day while orders concentrate at lunch and dinner. Capacity is an illustrative six orders per service hour, not a measured service standard.

**Business impact:** Peak pressure can affect experience while spare capacity raises cost per order. The exports identify the store, weekday and hour for a roster trial.

**Recommended action:** Workforce Planning: trial staggered starts and meal-peak overlap at two stores; keep total hours stable initially and compare service outcomes.

## 3. Target excess food waste with preparation controls

**What happened:** Gold Coast had Q4 waste of 7.8%, versus its location-peer median of 4.3%.

**Why:** The synthetic operation links preparation variability and management efficiency to waste. Product and reason exports support investigation of specific preparation practices.

**Business impact:** Matching the peer rate on the same ingredient-cost base suggests A$3,630 of quarterly waste-cost opportunity; feasibility is untested.

**Recommended action:** Restaurant Manager: pilot smaller batches and daily waste reason reviews. Track waste cost alongside stock-outs and satisfaction for four weeks.

## 4. Review the weakest promotion before repeating it

**What happened:** Spring Social produced estimated incremental revenue of A$-56,840 and incremental contribution of A$-210,901 after campaign costs; ROI was -1193%.

**Why:** This summary includes only campaigns with eligible controls; unavailable estimates and their spend are excluded together. Sales uplift must pay for discounts, ingredient costs, commissions, incremental labour and campaign spending. Matched controls provide an imperfect counterfactual.

**Business impact:** Repeating a weak offer can erode contribution despite an attractive sales headline. Campaign-specific confidence ranges may cross zero.

**Recommended action:** Marketing and Finance: use a randomised store trial to compare a smaller discount or bundle. Make contribution the primary success metric.

## 5. Use contribution and margin together in menu decisions

**What happened:** Mains generated 63.2% of gross contribution, while Drinks had the highest category ingredient margin.

**Why:** High-volume mains and lower-cost add-ons play different roles. Revenue alone does not capture ingredient economics.

**Business impact:** Menu placement and attachments can improve mix, but gross contribution excludes additional preparation labour and cannibalisation.

**Recommended action:** Operations and Marketing: test one complementary add-on bundle; evaluate basket contribution, uptake and service time.

## 6. Use early activity to prioritise retention experiments

**What happened:** Among fully observed customers, 85.7% of those with four or more early visits returned in days 31–90, versus 39.8% with one early visit.

**Why:** Early activity reflects customer preference and opportunity to visit. Redemption comparisons are stratified by activity and cannot establish loyalty causation.

**Business impact:** The eligible identified-customer 90-day repeat rate was 56.3%. Anonymous visitors are outside this denominator.

**Recommended action:** Loyalty Manager: test a second-visit message with a random holdout among low-activity new members; measure return rate and contribution.

## 7. Treat service pressure as an experience warning

**What happened:** Controlled service-pressure/satisfaction correlation was -0.25, with an approximate store-bootstrap interval of [-0.31, -0.20].

**Why:** The comparison adjusts for store, weekday, calendar month and order volume. Synthetic staffing mechanisms contribute to the relationship; selective survey responses remain a limitation.

**Business impact:** Service scores provide a guardrail against roster changes that improve labour ratios but overload busy periods.

**Recommended action:** Area Manager: review pressure and satisfaction together weekly, then evaluate roster pilots with pre-agreed service and cost measures.

## 8. Use evaluated forecasts for four-week planning

**What happened:** Calendar ridge was selected using validation MAE. Final holdout MAE was A$452 per store-day, RMSE A$587, and MAPE 14.7%.

**Why:** Its holdout MAE improvement over the seasonal naive comparator was 26.1%. Calendar and lag features provide information beyond a repeated weekly pattern when useful.

**Business impact:** The next 28 synthetic planning days total A$1,640,024. Forecast error should be considered before committing labour or stock.

**Recommended action:** Workforce Planning: combine daily forecasts with each store's observed basket value and hourly mix; revisit weekly and record overrides.
